# 4. From Aggregate Auditing to Reliable Patient-Level Risk Assessment

Throughout this review, **vulnerability** denotes an attack- and configuration-specific empirical quantity, such as per-patient MIA AUC or a likelihood-ratio statistic. The term **risk** is reserved, where possible, for a calibrated probability or for an explicitly defined operational decision. This distinction prevents AUCs, likelihood ratios, posterior probabilities, and reconstruction errors from being treated as interchangeable scales.

## 4.1 Aggregate Auditing: Capabilities and Limitations

Aggregate privacy auditing pools attack scores for member and non-member records and summarizes their separation using a single measure or curve, such as attack accuracy, ROC-AUC, or TPR at a selected FPR. Shokri et al.'s shadow-model framework established a practical black-box form of this audit by generating labeled “in” and “out” examples with shadow models and using them to train an attack classifier [1]. Aggregate summaries remain valuable for system-level screening and for controlled comparisons across target models, attacks, training procedures, and defenses. Their estimand, however, is attack performance for records drawn from the audit population; it is not the exposure of a specified record or patient.

Deployment-relevant interpretation therefore requires explicit operating points. Accuracy depends on the member–non-member balance and the decision threshold, whereas ROC-AUC averages discrimination across all FPRs, including regions that may be unacceptable in practice. Carlini et al. recommend reporting the full ROC curve on logarithmic axes and the TPR at low FPRs, such as 0.1% or 0.001%, to reveal attacks that confidently identify a small subset of members [2]. Such estimates require sufficient non-member observations. If approximately half of $M$ target-model observations for each sample fall under the out hypothesis, at least $2/\alpha$ models are required merely to attain an empirical FPR of $\alpha$; even at this minimum, the ROC estimate may remain highly uncertain [14]. This is a resolution bound rather than a sufficient-sample recommendation. Likewise, observing no false positives in a small audit does not establish a zero population FPR and should be accompanied by an appropriate binomial confidence bound.

The clinical limitation of aggregation is demonstrated directly by Knolle et al. Across multiple diagnostic datasets and models, aggregate ROC curves and AUCs remained close to random-guessing performance, while record-specific analyses over repeated target models identified a small subset of patients with near-perfect membership distinguishability [3]. For patients contributing multiple records, the study defined the patient-level summary as the maximum record-level MIA AUC, reflecting the premise that successful inference for any contributed record may reveal the patient's participation. Thus, a reassuring global AUC can coexist with severe exposure in the extreme-risk tail because the majority of low-risk records dominates the pooled result. For the same reason, an aggregate improvement after a defense does not establish that the most vulnerable patients benefited.

Pooled scores can also be confounded by intrinsic sample difficulty. An easy non-member may receive high confidence or low loss from many models and consequently resemble a member, whereas a difficult member may not. Watson et al. calibrate a target score against the behavior of reference models trained on data from the same distribution, thereby separating target-model-specific behavior from general predictability and reducing false positives [13]. Per-example reference distributions in likelihood-ratio attacks serve a related purpose [2]. These methods improve the evidential meaning of an attack score, but calibration of a score does not by itself convert a pooled audit into an estimate of individual vulnerability.

Aggregate findings are additionally conditional on population composition and on the membership experiment being implemented. Group prevalence, member and non-member sampling, threshold selection, and randomness in data construction and model training can all change the reported result. Ye et al. show that fixing or randomly drawing the target model, record, surrounding dataset, and training randomness defines different membership games and therefore different leakage quantities [6]. Subgroup estimates add another layer of uncertainty: Kulynych et al. demonstrate that naïve procedures can overstate disparity, particularly for small groups, and propose repeated-measures estimation with statistical testing and multiple-comparison correction [7]. Jälkö et al. further show that concatenating scores across individuals under one global threshold controls only the average per-sample FPR; individual FPRs may vary substantially, and heterogeneity may be suppressed [14]. Because this latter evidence is currently a methodological preprint, it should be treated as an important caution pending peer review.

Aggregate auditing is therefore necessary for system-level screening and standardized comparison, but it is insufficient to identify who bears the risk, how large the extreme-risk tail is, or whether a defense protects those at greatest risk. Answering these questions requires record-level and sample-specific assessment.

## 4.2 Record-Level and Sample-Specific Vulnerability

Moving from aggregate auditing to individual assessment changes both the unit and the meaning of analysis. A membership score for one record against one trained model is a single observation whose interpretation depends on the attack signal, threshold, membership prior, and randomness in data sampling and training. It is therefore insufficient for assigning a stable “high-risk” label. Record-level assessment should instead characterize how distinguishable the model's behavior is when the same record is included in, rather than excluded from, training, using repeated target models or appropriately constructed reference distributions. High vulnerability is consequently defined relative to a specified counterfactual experiment; it is not an intrinsic, attack-independent property of the record.

Song and Mittal introduced an early fine-grained privacy-risk score that formalizes part of this perspective [15]. For a sample $z$, the score is the posterior probability

\[
P\!\left(z\in D_{\mathrm{tr}}\mid O(F,z)\right),
\]

after observing the target model's behavior $O(F,z)$ on that sample. By Bayes' rule, the score depends on the prior probability of membership and on the conditional output distributions for members and non-members. Their experiments used equal membership priors and estimated class-conditional distributions from shadow-model outputs on shadow training and test data, with modified entropy as one possible statistic. The analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity and showed that a defense could reduce average privacy risk while increasing risk for a small subset of samples. Nevertheless, this is a fine-grained posterior score rather than a fully sample-specific counterfactual distribution: it depends on the membership prior, the selected statistic, and the quality and similarity of the shadow data. The evidence was also obtained primarily from general benchmarks, including a health-related tabular dataset, rather than from patient-level clinical audits.

LiRA advances sample-specific assessment by constructing a separate membership comparison for each target example [2]. Reference models trained with and without a fixed example estimate its “in” and “out” distributions of transformed confidence or loss, and the observation from the target model is evaluated through a likelihood ratio. Unlike a single global threshold, this comparison accounts for variation in baseline difficulty and for the effect of including the particular record. Ye et al. show why the underlying experiment must still be stated: fixing or marginalizing over the target record, model, dataset, and training randomness produces distinct membership games and leakage estimands [6]. Watson et al. address a related confound by calibrating target-model loss against typical reference-model behavior, so that an intrinsically easy non-member is not considered vulnerable solely because the model predicts it confidently [13]. In this sense, sample-specific vulnerability is a counterfactual comparison, not a ranking of uncalibrated raw scores.

Knolle et al. provide the central clinical demonstration of this approach [3]. They trained 200 target models on random patient subsets and, for each clinical record, partitioned logit-transformed confidence values according to whether the record was included in or excluded from training. Assuming Gaussian in and out distributions, they calculated a bi-normal record-level MIA AUC. This quantity is the probability that the attack statistic from a model trained with the record exceeds the statistic from a model trained without it. Across the investigated clinical datasets, the repeated-training design exposed an extreme-risk tail containing records with near-perfect membership distinguishability even when aggregate AUCs remained close to random guessing. Clinically important vulnerability can therefore be concentrated in particular records and concealed by population averages.

Record-level estimates nevertheless remain conditional measurements rather than universal privacy labels. They can change with the attack, observable signal, adversarial access, membership prior, learning algorithm, data-sampling protocol, reference-model construction, number of repetitions, and assumptions imposed on the in and out distributions. Efficient reuse of a finite population of records may also distort the estimated distributions: Jälkö et al. identify a finite-population effect in an efficient LiRA implementation that can underestimate distributional variance and positively bias per-sample vulnerability estimates [14]. These dependencies should be documented before records are compared or classified as highly vulnerable. Moreover, an estimate for one released model addresses exposure under that realization, whereas an estimate averaged across retraining runs addresses vulnerability of the learning procedure. Neither should be silently substituted for the other.

The analysis so far treats one record as the unit of exposure. In clinical data, however, one person may contribute several related visits, scans, waveforms, or measurements. Section 4.3 therefore considers how record-level evidence should be translated into a patient-level membership event.

## 4.3 Patient-Level Membership and Multiple-Record Contributions

Patient-level membership requires redefining the protected event rather than merely relabelling a record-level score. Let $R_p=\{r_{p1},\ldots,r_{pm_p}\}$ denote the set of records associated with patient $p$, and let $D$ denote the training records. An any-contribution game tests

\[
H_0:D\cap R_p=\varnothing
\qquad\text{versus}\qquad
H_1:D\cap R_p\neq\varnothing.
\]

This asks whether the patient contributed at least one training record. It differs from a whole-contribution game,

\[
H_0:D\cap R_p=\varnothing
\qquad\text{versus}\qquad
H_1:R_p\subseteq D,
\]

in which partial-contribution states are excluded by the experimental design. A threshold-contribution game may instead test $H_0:|D\cap R_p|<k$ against $H_1:|D\cap R_p|\geq k$. These hypotheses protect different secrets and are not interchangeable. Patient-level splitting assigns one membership state to all of a patient's records, whereas record-level splitting permits partial membership and may also leak patient information across training and evaluation partitions.

Redefining the hypothesis changes how record-level evidence can be combined. For one model and one attack score $s$, the statistic

\[
T_p(F)=\max_j s(F,r_{pj})
\]

is aligned with the any-contribution threat because one confidently inferred record may reveal the patient's participation. However, the maximum increases with the number of opportunities and may favor patients with more records even when their record-score distributions are identical. A mean represents the vulnerability of a typical record but may conceal a single revealing examination, while majority voting may miss sparse but decisive evidence. Repeated scans, visits, or measurements from one patient are also correlated, so multiplying record-level probabilities or combining tests under independence can produce unjustified confidence. A patient-level statistic therefore needs a prespecified decision rule and a null distribution that reflects record count, within-patient dependence, and the intended membership event.

It is also important to distinguish a within-attack patient statistic from a worst-record audit summary. The quantity $\max_j s(F,r_{pj})$ combines record scores observed for a particular model, whereas

\[
\max_j \widehat{\mathrm{AUC}}_{pj}
\]

selects the largest estimated record-level AUC across repeated models. The latter describes the patient's most distinguishable record under the audit; it is not automatically the AUC of a joint patient-level attack, nor is it a calibrated probability that the patient was a member. Conflating these quantities can make a descriptive worst-record summary appear to be a formally evaluated patient-level classifier.

Knolle et al. operationalised an any-record interpretation in clinical AI by treating successful inference for any contributed record as sufficient to reveal patient membership and using the maximum record-level MIA AUC as the patient-level summary [3]. Their target models were trained on patient-level subsets, ensuring that all records belonging to a patient shared the same inclusion state. This conservative summary exposed patients with near-perfect record vulnerability that aggregate auditing obscured. It also demonstrates why patient-level sampling matters: the authors report that record-level sampling underestimated MIA success for patients contributing multiple similar records. Nevertheless, their maximum-AUC summary can depend on record multiplicity and does not establish a universally optimal rule for combining patient evidence.

Evidence outside clinical AI further shows why attacks designed directly for a subject-level hypothesis may be preferable to post hoc aggregation. Suri et al. define subject membership by distinguishing training populations that exclude a person from populations containing that person's data, including settings in which the records are distributed across institutions [16]. Their black-box attacks can use samples from the subject's distribution without requiring the exact records used for training, separating subject privacy from both item-level membership and the privacy of a participating institution. Li et al. similarly ask whether any image from a user trained a metric-embedding model [17]. Their attack exploits the compactness of embeddings from several same-user images and can infer a training user using images that were not themselves training records. These studies show that person-level evidence may arise from shared distributional structure across records. Because neither study evaluates clinical patients, however, they provide methodological support rather than direct estimates of clinical risk.

Patient-level vulnerability is therefore determined by the membership hypothesis, contribution mechanism, number and dependence of records, attack access, and evidence-combination rule. More records may create more opportunities for an extreme score or provide a stronger subject-level signal, but available findings do not establish a universally monotonic relationship. A rigorous audit should report the patient-sampling unit, distinguish complete from partial contribution, preserve patient linkage when constructing member and non-member partitions, calibrate the selected patient statistic for record multiplicity and dependence, and compare post hoc aggregation with attacks designed directly for the patient-level hypothesis. Once this estimand is fixed, the next question is whether it can be estimated reliably.

## 4.4 Estimating Risk: Uncertainty, Calibration, and Reproducibility

Once the record or patient has been fixed as the unit of analysis, its estimated vulnerability remains a random quantity. Variation can arise from sampling the surrounding dataset, assigning membership, initializing the model, data augmentation, and stochastic optimization. Ye et al. show that fixing or randomly drawing the target record, target model, surrounding dataset, and training randomness defines different membership games and quantities [6]. A measurement obtained from one seed therefore characterizes one model realization; it does not by itself establish stable vulnerability of the learning procedure. Patient-level repetitions must also preserve the declared contribution mechanism: all records should share membership when the game concerns whole-patient inclusion, whereas partial contribution requires a separately specified hypothesis and sampling design.

Statistical precision depends on the number of effectively independent observations under the in and out hypotheses. Knolle et al. used 200 target models, evenly split between the two hypotheses for every record, and calculated the standard error of each bi-normal record-level AUC [3]. Fewer repetitions produce greater uncertainty, particularly for estimates close to a classification threshold. Estimation is even more demanding at low FPRs. With approximately half of $M$ target-model observations under the out hypothesis, Jälkö et al. note that at least $2/\alpha$ total models are required merely to attain an empirical FPR of $\alpha$; meeting this resolution bound does not make the estimated ROC curve reliable [14]. A zero observed false-positive count should therefore be reported with an upper confidence bound rather than interpreted as zero population FPR. Nominal model counts may also overstate information when models repeatedly reuse overlapping subsets of a finite data pool. Sensitivity to resampling design and finite-population correction should be assessed where relevant.

Calibration is distinct from precision and has several meanings in this literature. First, operating-point calibration asks whether the declared FPR is achieved for the unit being classified. A single threshold applied to concatenated scores can control the average per-sample FPR while individual samples have widely different FPRs [14]. Second, difficulty calibration compares a target-model score with typical reference-model behavior so that intrinsic predictability is not mistaken for membership evidence [13]. Third, posterior-probability calibration asks whether a reported privacy-risk score corresponds to the stated membership probability under an explicit prior [15]. Per-example likelihood ratios address heterogeneity in evidence, but they do not by themselves prove posterior calibration. Patient-level aggregation adds a further requirement: calibrated record scores do not guarantee that their maximum, mean, or joint statistic is calibrated for patients with different record counts and dependence structures. Calibration must therefore be evaluated at the same unit and with the same decision rule used to assign risk.

Reproducibility should also be distinguished from robustness. Reproducibility asks whether the same prespecified audit—using the same estimand, attack, sampling design, and analysis procedure—produces compatible conclusions when rerun. Robustness asks whether the conclusion persists under defensible changes to seeds, architectures, training procedures, attack signals, reference data, and adversarial-knowledge assumptions. Failure of robustness does not necessarily invalidate an audit, because different attacks or membership games may estimate different quantities; it instead limits the scope of the resulting claim. Borderline cases whose classification changes within statistical uncertainty should remain indeterminate rather than being forced into binary categories.

At minimum, an individual-risk report should state the membership hypothesis, sampling unit, attack access and signal, construction of target and reference models, numbers of in and out observations, random seeds, operating FPR, calibration method, point estimate, confidence interval, and decision rule. Sensitivity analyses should examine the number of models, distributional assumptions, reference data, patient aggregation rule, and relevant training or attack choices. If formal high-risk labels are assigned simultaneously to many patients, simultaneous uncertainty or an appropriate multiple-testing procedure should be considered. This individual-level multiplicity problem should not be attributed directly to subgroup studies. At the subgroup level, Kulynych et al. specifically show that small groups can yield biased or unstable disparity estimates and advocate repeated-measures, bias-aware estimation with statistical testing and multiple-comparison correction [7].

A defensible high-risk designation is therefore one whose uncertainty is quantified, whose calibration matches the unit of protection, and whose interpretation survives prespecified reproducibility checks. Section 4.5 considers how such evidence should be integrated when identifying high-risk patients across models and attacks.

## 4.5 Identifying High-Risk Patients Across Models and Attacks

Having defined the unit of exposure and the requirements for estimating its vulnerability, the next task is to determine when evidence supports describing a patient as high risk and whether that conclusion persists across plausible models and attacks. No reviewed study establishes a universally validated clinical threshold for high privacy risk. Any designation is therefore operational, metric-specific, and conditional on the declared membership game. At least four rules are possible. A metric-specific absolute threshold classifies a patient when a prespecified patient-level summary exceeds a fixed value; Knolle et al., for example, treated a maximum record-level MIA AUC of at least 0.95 as near-perfect patient-level attack success [3]. An operating-point definition may instead require a high TPR for a patient-level statistic at a prespecified patient-level FPR, extending Carlini et al.'s low-FPR reporting principle to the patient unit [2]. A relative definition selects, for example, the highest-risk 1% of patients, while a conservative inferential rule requires the lower confidence bound—not only the point estimate—to exceed a threshold. These definitions are not interchangeable. In particular, a top-1% rule necessarily produces an extreme set even when all absolute risks are low and cannot by itself demonstrate a meaningful privacy breach.

For claims about a learning procedure, recurrence across retraining runs provides stronger evidence than exceedance in one fitted realization. Model-specific exposure remains important for the model actually deployed, but it should not be generalized to other seeds, architectures, capacities, or defenses without repeated evidence. Knolle et al. showed that increasing model capacity could substantially enlarge the extreme-vulnerability tail [3]. This establishes model dependence in the prevalence of exposure, not persistence of the same patient identities across capacities. Cross-institution comparisons are more limited still: unless patient identities are legitimately linkable and the estimand is unchanged, they compare the prevalence or determinants of vulnerability rather than persistence of individual exposure.

Consistency should also be examined across attacks that target the same membership event under comparable access assumptions. Loss-based attacks, LiRA, RMIA, and related procedures can be compared through rank correlations, threshold-exceedance frequencies, and overlap between prespecified patient sets. Disagreement is not automatically measurement error: attacks may exploit different signals or retain different adversarial uncertainties [6]. A union of attack-specific sets is sensitive but increases false-positive designations unless multiplicity is controlled; an intersection is conservative and can omit genuine attack-specific exposure. Neither should be presented as a universal high-risk label.

Comparability becomes more restricted when the audit extends beyond membership inference. MIA AUC, attribute-inference accuracy beyond an auxiliary-information baseline, and inversion or reconstruction error target different secrets and should not be normalized and averaged without a validated decision-theoretic justification [4]. A more defensible representation is a multidimensional profile,

\[
\mathbf{V}_p=
\left(
V_p^{\mathrm{MIA}},
V_p^{\mathrm{attribute}},
V_p^{\mathrm{inversion}},
V_p^{\mathrm{reconstruction}}
\right),
\]

with the targeted secret, attack access, operating point, attribution to the patient, and uncertainty reported separately for each dimension. Model stealing should remain outside this patient-disclosure profile unless an empirical link to disclosure of patient information is demonstrated.

Evidence strength is best described along two axes: recurrence across prespecified retraining conditions and support across compatible attacks. The strongest claim—recurrent cross-model and cross-attack exposure—requires attacks that target the same secret and membership hypothesis under comparable threat models. These axes characterize robustness, not severity. Calibration must also match the patient-level decision rule. Jälkö et al. show at sample level that a pooled threshold can mask heterogeneous sample-specific FPRs [14]; extending this concern to patient statistics is a methodological implication that still requires direct validation.

Accordingly, studies should report the decision rule, estimate and confidence interval, evaluated conditions, recurrence frequency, cross-run agreement, set overlap, and multiplicity adjustment. A patient selected in one condition should not be called persistently vulnerable, although that model-specific exposure may still matter. Only after elevated vulnerability exceeds uncertainty—and its scope across models and attacks is stated accurately—can the review ask whether exposed patients are concentrated within particular clinical or demographic populations. Section 5 examines that evidence.
