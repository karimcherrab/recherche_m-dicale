# 5. Evidence of Disparate Privacy Across Clinical Populations

## 5.1 Underrepresented Populations and Rare Clinical Conditions

Underrepresentation, clinical rarity, and record atypicality are related but should not be treated as interchangeable. An underrepresented group contributes a small number or proportion of observations to the model’s training data. A rare condition is a diagnosis, phenotype, or clinical state with low prevalence in the relevant population or dataset. An atypical record, by contrast, occupies a low-density or unusual region of the feature distribution and may belong to either a minority or majority group. A rare condition may generate relatively typical records within its clinical subgroup, whereas a common condition can contain highly atypical individual presentations. Evidence concerning these three properties should therefore be assessed separately.

Knolle et al. provide the most direct clinical evidence linking limited representation to extreme membership vulnerability [3]. Across seven medical datasets, they estimated record-level MIA AUC using 200 repeatedly trained target models and defined the extreme-risk tail as the highest 1% of record-level AUC values. They then compared the subgroup composition of this tail with the composition expected from the complete training data. Significant differences were observed for several stratifications, including disease status, self-reported race, sex, insurance status, and imaging protocol. In MIMIC-IV-ED, records from Black patients and patients with Medicaid insurance occurred 31% and 126% more frequently than expected in the tail, respectively. Across all subgroup comparisons, large positive Pearson residuals occurred mainly among groups contributing less than 20% of the records, and group size was weakly to moderately inversely correlated with tail enrichment [3]. These results support an association between limited representation and disproportionate extreme record-level vulnerability.

The evidence concerning rare diseases and clinical states is more heterogeneous. In EMBED, benign findings classified as BI-RADS-2 and findings suspicious of malignancy classified as BI-RADS-4 were overrepresented in the extreme tail by 60% and 1,179%, respectively; almost entirely fatty and extremely dense breasts were also disproportionately represented [3]. However, the model predicted breast density rather than tumour status, and the reported racial differences in this dataset may themselves be confounded by density. In MIMIC-IV-ED, cancer records were 18% more frequent than expected in the tail, but the cancer comparison was not statistically significant after correction. Statistical significance could not be evaluated for disease-label comparisons in CheXpert and MIMIC-CXR because patients could have multiple, non-mutually-exclusive diagnoses. Conversely, marked disease-related composition differences were reported in datasets such as FairVision and Fitzpatrick 17k. Collectively, these findings indicate dataset-specific associations, but do not establish that rare diagnoses universally produce higher privacy risk.

Nonclinical and health-administrative studies provide supporting, rather than directly transferable, evidence. Kulynych et al. defined subgroup vulnerability as normalized membership advantage within a population group and introduced repeated-measures tests for comparing it reliably [7]. On Adult, smaller racial groups such as Asian-Pac-Islander and “Other” exhibited significant disparities under neural-network models. On Texas-50K, which was derived from hospital-discharge records, nearly all subgroup pairs differed under the 32-neuron network. Nevertheless, standard logistic regression did not show significant disparity, demonstrating that representation alone was insufficient and that model family and subgroup-specific generalization mattered. Chang and Shokri further manipulated group proportions in synthetic data and found that reducing the size of an unprivileged group increased its fairness-induced privacy cost [18]. Their Law, Bank, and COMPAS experiments likewise showed that privacy effects varied with the protected attribute, model capacity, and fairness constraint. These are record-level benchmark results and should not be presented as evidence about rare clinical conditions.

Several alternative explanations constrain causal interpretation. Smaller groups may have higher loss, poorer predictive performance, greater feature variability, or different label distributions. Disease, demographics, insurance, and acquisition protocol may also overlap, making marginal subgroup comparisons difficult to interpret. Moreover, Knolle et al.’s disparity analysis used records, although patients may contribute different numbers of correlated records. Let \(T\) denote membership in the selected extreme-risk tail. Composition tests compare \(P(G=g\mid T)\) with the group’s overall representation \(P(G=g)\). With the same record as the unit, enrichment also implies an increased probability \(P(T\mid G=g)\) for that particular tail, but it does not show that every group member has elevated continuous risk or that patient-level risk is higher when record multiplicity differs. The relative top-1% definition also creates an extreme tail in every model, irrespective of its absolute security. Small subgroup counts, repeated comparisons, and threshold choice introduce additional uncertainty, although Knolle et al. applied row-wise Bonferroni correction and Kulynych et al. used ANOVA with multiplicity-adjusted post-hoc tests [3,7].

Overall, the available evidence supports a recurring but model- and dataset-dependent association between underrepresentation and extreme record-level membership vulnerability. It does not yet establish a general causal effect of group size or clinical rarity on patient-level risk. The next section therefore examines whether similar disparities appear across demographic and intersecting populations, where representation, clinical status, and social attributes may operate jointly.

## 5.2 Demographic and Intersecting Subgroups

Evidence of demographic disparity in membership vulnerability must distinguish among three analytically different comparisons. A marginal analysis compares groups defined by one attribute, such as race, sex, or insurance status. A label-conditional analysis compares an attribute within levels of the clinical outcome or prediction label. Genuine intersectional analysis instead evaluates combinations of two or more social or demographic attributes, such as race × sex × insurance status. This distinction matters because a protected attribute combined with a target label does not constitute a demographic intersection. Moreover, race, ethnicity, and insurance status should be interpreted as socially and institutionally situated variables rather than biological patient characteristics.

Knolle et al. provide the strongest direct clinical evidence of demographic variation in privacy risk [3]. Across seven clinical datasets, they examined whether records belonging to groups defined separately by self-reported race, sex, insurance status, disease status, or acquisition protocol appeared more frequently than expected in the highest 1% of record-level MIA AUC values. In MIMIC-IV-ED, for example, records from Black patients and Medicaid-covered patients were respectively 31% and 126% more prevalent in this extreme-risk tail than expected from their representation in the complete training data. Positive Pearson residuals also occurred frequently among groups contributing less than 20% of a dataset, although the direction and statistical significance of the differences were not uniform across datasets or attributes. Importantly, this analysis describes the composition of a record-level extreme-risk tail. It does not directly estimate the probability that a patient from a given demographic group will be successfully identified at a prespecified false-positive rate. The attributes were primarily examined one at a time, and repeated records from the same patient could affect group representation. Thus, the study supports demographic heterogeneity in clinical privacy exposure but not comprehensive patient-level intersectional disparity.

Health-administrative and nonclinical studies provide supporting, but less directly transferable, evidence. Kulynych et al. found statistically significant racial differences in normalized membership-inference advantage for some neural-network configurations on the Adult and Texas-50K hospital-discharge datasets [7]. However, disparities varied with model family and were often absent for logistic regression, indicating that demographic vulnerability was not an invariant property of a group. Zhong et al. similarly evaluated gender and race separately, conditioning each attribute on the binary target label [19]. On Adult, aggregate MIA accuracy was 54.1%, whereas accuracy reached 69.8% for one race–label subgroup; the largest corresponding between-group gap was 12.5 percentage points. Smaller groups were often more vulnerable, but exceptions occurred, and group size alone did not explain the results. The Hospital dataset supplied a health-related administrative setting, yet its unit remained a discharge record rather than a patient, and neither study evaluated intersections among multiple demographic attributes.

Research on algorithmic fairness further shows that subgroup conclusions depend on the attack and operating point. Chang and Shokri demonstrated that fairness interventions could change loss-based membership vulnerability differently across protected-attribute–label groups [18]. Their subgroup thresholds, however, assumed substantial attacker knowledge, and their experiments were not clinical. Purkait et al. subsequently evaluated subgroup-tailored likelihood-ratio attacks at very low FPRs [21]. In Bank Marketing, subgroup-restricted sampling produced a TPR of 13.6% at an observed 0% FPR for one subgroup, compared with 12.2% using population sampling; under adversarial debiasing, the corresponding values were 9.1% and 3.5%. These results show that population-level attacks can miss subgroup-specific leakage. Nevertheless, parameters were optimized separately for each subgroup, and the subgroup method underperformed LiRA when a single global configuration was imposed. The study therefore informs audit design rather than establishing clinical or intersectional prevalence.

The principal evidence gap is consequently not the absence of any demographic differences, but the scarcity of well-powered clinical studies estimating patient-level risk across genuine demographic intersections. Intersectional cells can become extremely small, producing unstable attack rates and multiple-comparison problems. Herlihy et al., although studying predictive performance rather than privacy, illustrate this challenge: in their diabetes evaluation, 8 of 32 race–sex–age groups contained fewer than ten observations and almost half contained fewer than 25 [20]. Their structured-regression and confidence-interval framework offers a potential methodological direction, but it requires separate validation for membership-risk outcomes and correlated patient records. Future privacy audits should prespecify clinically justified intersections, report group counts and missingness, preserve a common membership experiment, calibrate FPR within relevant groups, account for multiple records per patient, and provide confidence intervals and multiplicity-adjusted comparisons across training seeds and attacks. Disease prevalence, institutional composition, and model utility should also be examined as competing explanations rather than attributed automatically to demographic identity.

Taken together, the evidence supports model- and attack-dependent demographic variation in record-level membership vulnerability, but remains insufficient to establish robust patient-level intersectional disparities. Determining whether observed differences reflect demographic position, institutional context, or data-acquisition processes therefore requires the setting- and protocol-specific analysis developed in Section 5.3.

## 5.3 Clinical Settings, Institutions, and Acquisition Protocols

Clinical context can enter a privacy audit at three related but distinct levels. The first is the institution or site, including the hospital, clinical centre, geographical region, or dataset source. The second is acquisition, encompassing scanner or device vendor, imaging view, scan settings, sampling frequency, and ECG lead configuration. The third is clinical workflow, such as inpatient versus outpatient care, emergency versus scheduled assessment, screening versus diagnostic imaging, and the period in which data were collected. These factors may change what the target model learns, the baseline difficulty of individual records, and the correspondence between target and reference data. Consequently, an attack may separate members from non-members because training induced record-specific behavior, because the two groups originate from different clinical environments, or through a combination of both. Attack separability therefore should not be interpreted as pure membership leakage without examining site and acquisition confounding.

Knolle et al. provide direct clinical evidence that acquisition context is associated with the distribution of extreme membership vulnerability [3]. Their subgroup audit compared the representation of imaging protocols within the highest 1% of record-level MIA AUC values with their representation in the complete training dataset. The investigated categories included anteroposterior and posteroanterior views in CheXpert; anteroposterior, posteroanterior, lateral, and left-lateral views in MIMIC-CXR; and mediolateral-oblique and craniocaudal views in EMBED. Two-sided chi-squared tests, Pearson residuals, and row-wise Bonferroni correction identified significant compositional differences for several protocol comparisons. These findings establish protocol-associated heterogeneity in the extreme-risk tail, but do not isolate acquisition protocol as a causal driver of privacy leakage. Imaging view is related to patient mobility, disease severity, examination location, and diagnostic workflow; its association with risk may also reflect protocol frequency and disease composition. Moreover, the analysis concerns vulnerable records rather than the probability that a patient will be identified under a fixed operating point.

The distinction between membership and institutional origin becomes particularly important when members and non-members come from different datasets. Wang et al. evaluated self-supervised ECG encoders using subject-level aggregation and reported AUC, TPR at a 1% FPR, and attacker advantage [22]. Leakage varied substantially across training objectives and cohorts and was especially pronounced in some small, institution-specific datasets. However, non-members were sampled from ECG datasets disjoint from the pretraining dataset. The authors acknowledge that the resulting signal may combine subject participation with acquisition hardware, voltage scaling, sensor noise, resampling, or other dataset-specific properties; they accordingly interpret the results as cross-dataset participation leakage rather than pure within-dataset subject identification. Humphries et al. demonstrate the same identification problem more directly [23]. When a target model was trained using heart-disease records from Cleveland, Hungary, Switzerland, or VA Long Beach and records from the other hospitals were treated as non-members, membership advantage was higher than under an IID construction formed by shuffling the hospital datasets. The attack may therefore be exploiting a property shared by records from the training hospital. Such site recognition can itself reveal sensitive institutional or cohort participation, but it does not answer the narrower question of whether a particular patient participated among other patients from the same institution.

Data-partition artifacts introduce a related threat to validity. Bai et al. evaluated an attack involving synthetic brain MRI images generated by StyleGAN3 and added a member-only negative control in which the classifier distinguished two disjoint subsets of training patients, despite both subsets having identical membership status [24]. The control achieved higher AUC than the primary member–non-member experiment in both the small setting (0.8421 versus 0.8216) and the large setting (0.9768 versus 0.9655), with bootstrap confidence intervals for both differences excluding zero. This result shows that high discrimination may arise from learnable partition structure rather than model leakage. Its direct generalizability is limited because it concerns a generative model and synthetic MRI matching, but the negative-control principle applies broadly. Shi et al. further show that mismatch between target and shadow distributions has no uniform effect: cutout, jitter, Gaussian noise, label shift, and attribute shift could either increase or decrease attack performance, and the same shift could have opposite effects for different MIAs [25]. Thus, lower success under site shift does not necessarily demonstrate stronger privacy, while higher success does not by itself establish patient memorization.

Reliable multi-institutional auditing should therefore include complementary comparisons: within-site members versus within-site non-members; pooled global versus site-conditioned attacks; global versus site- or protocol-matched reference models; and primary attacks versus member-only, shuffled-membership, or membership-label permutation controls. Audits should additionally assess whether conclusions persist after matching or adjustment for disease status, demographic composition, clinical workflow, acquisition protocol, and the number of records contributed per patient. Reporting site-specific sample sizes, preprocessing pipelines, attack thresholds, and confidence intervals is necessary to determine whether differences exceed sampling uncertainty. Under this framework, cross-dataset leakage should be reported as a meaningful but distinct outcome rather than treated as interchangeable with within-site patient membership. Even after institutional and acquisition-related differences are controlled, however, site-level averages cannot determine whether vulnerability is distributed broadly or concentrated in a few individuals. Section 5.4 therefore examines within-group variation and extreme-risk patients.

## 5.4 Within-Group Variation and Extreme-Risk Patients

Group-level disparity and within-group heterogeneity answer different questions. Between-group disparity concerns differences in average vulnerability or in the composition of a high-risk tail across populations, whereas within-group variation concerns how widely risk differs among patients assigned to the same population. An extreme-risk tail denotes the upper part of an estimated risk distribution rather than an intrinsic or permanent patient characteristic. Its membership is conditional on the attack, target model, membership hypothesis, unit of analysis, reference distribution, training repetitions, and prespecified threshold or percentile. Consequently, a subgroup with modest average risk may still contain highly exposed patients, while a subgroup with elevated average risk may include many patients whose estimated vulnerability remains close to chance.

The strongest direct clinical evidence comes from Knolle et al. [3]. Across seven clinical datasets, the authors trained 200 target models on varying training subsets and estimated a record-level MIA AUC for each record. Patient-level exposure was then summarized using the maximum record-level MIA AUC among the records contributed by that patient. Empirical survival functions revealed a small tail of patients with near-perfect distinguishability even when aggregate attack AUC remained close to random guessing, demonstrating marked heterogeneity that system-level averages failed to represent. This finding nevertheless requires three qualifications. First, taking a maximum creates more opportunities for an extreme value as record count increases; patient-level tail membership may therefore be partly influenced by record multiplicity, although this effect was not separately quantified in the study. Second, the subgroup analysis of the highest 1% was conducted at the record level and therefore does not directly estimate patient-level tail probability. Third, the study did not formally decompose total variability into within-group and between-group components. It therefore supports substantial patient-level heterogeneity, but not the stronger claim that within-group variation exceeds between-group disparity.

Group identity alone is consequently insufficient for identifying an extreme-risk patient. Let \(\mathcal{T}\) denote the prespecified extreme-risk tail and \(R\) the risk estimate for the declared unit of analysis. Tail composition measures

\[
P(G=g\mid R\in\mathcal{T}),
\]

whereas within-group tail risk measures

\[
P(R\in\mathcal{T}\mid G=g).
\]

These quantities are related through group prevalence but are not interchangeable. A large group may constitute a substantial portion of the tail even when each member has a relatively low tail probability; conversely, a small group may have high within-group tail risk while contributing few patients to the tail. Moreover, absence of group overrepresentation does not imply absence of extremely vulnerable individuals. Kulynych et al. show that naïvely applying membership attacks can overestimate subgroup disparity and provide a statistical framework for testing such differences [7]. This concern is especially consequential when subgroup counts are small.

Evidence from general machine-learning benchmarks supports sample-level heterogeneity but cannot establish its clinical magnitude. Song and Mittal found that privacy-risk scores varied markedly across individual samples and that aggregate attack results could conceal highly exposed examples [15]. Carlini et al. similarly showed that low-FPR evaluation can reveal examples identifiable with high confidence even when average-case performance appears limited [2]. Watson et al. add an important interpretive constraint: an unusually high raw membership score may reflect an intrinsically easy or difficult sample rather than training-specific exposure, so difficulty calibration is necessary before ranking patients [13]. Jälkö et al. further caution, in a methodological preprint, that concatenating scores across individuals does not necessarily calibrate the FPR for each sample and that efficient LiRA estimation may exhibit positive finite-population bias [14]. These studies support the plausibility of extreme sample-specific risk, but their benchmark results should not be extrapolated directly to patients.

Whether the same patients consistently occupy the tail remains unresolved. Jebreel et al. found on nonclinical benchmarks that LiRA vulnerable sets obtained by thresholding at extremely low FPRs reproduced poorly across seeds and training variations, whereas likelihood-ratio rankings were comparatively more stable [26]. This suggests that repeated continuous rankings may be more defensible than declaring a fixed binary list of high-risk patients from one audit. Clinical studies should therefore report overall and within-group risk distributions, empirical survival curves, prespecified tail probabilities, confidence intervals, and the number of records contributed by each patient. The reproducibility measures described in Section 4.4 should then be applied to determine whether tail membership persists across runs, models, and attacks. The available evidence supports substantial patient-level heterogeneity, but does not yet establish that the same individuals consistently occupy the extreme-risk tail across attacks, models, and clinical settings. Section 5.5 examines the consistency of these findings and the limits of their generalization.

## 5.5 Consistency, Conflicting Findings, and Limits of Generalization

Consistency has at least three meanings in this literature. Within-study consistency asks whether a finding persists across datasets, models, training repetitions, or attacks under one protocol. Between-study consistency requires independent investigations to recover comparable patterns, whereas transportability asks whether a result remains valid in a new institution, population, or deployment setting. These levels should not be conflated. Knolle et al. observed privacy heterogeneity across several clinical datasets [3], but repetition within one coordinated study is not external replication. Moreover, membership-inference evaluations may implement different experiments and uncertainty sources [6]; comparison is meaningful only when the protected secret, membership hypothesis, access assumptions, and estimand are sufficiently aligned.

Several broad findings nevertheless converge. Aggregate attack performance can conceal a small tail of highly exposed records or patients; vulnerability varies among examples and sometimes among population groups; and its magnitude depends on the model, training procedure, and attack [2,3,7,15]. Song and Mittal demonstrated sample-specific heterogeneity on general benchmarks [15], while Carlini et al. showed why low-FPR evaluation can reveal risks obscured by average accuracy or ROC-AUC [2]. Kulynych et al. established that subgroup disparity is possible but requires appropriate statistical estimation [7]. Clinically, Knolle et al. identified near-perfect distinguishability for a minority of patients despite near-random aggregate performance, together with non-uniform subgroup representation in the extreme-risk tail [3]. However, direct patient-level clinical evidence still rests mainly on this study; other work primarily supports the plausibility or methodology of heterogeneity rather than independently replicating its clinical magnitude.

Agreement weakens regarding the direction and identity of disparity. Underrepresentation is not a universal predictor: in Knolle et al., associations varied across datasets, attributes, and subgroup comparisons, and not every stratification was significant [3]. Kulynych et al. found meaningful disparities but warned that naïve application of existing attacks may overestimate them [7]. Similarly, Chang and Shokri [18], Zhong et al. [19], and Purkait et al. [21] do not establish a fixed ranking of protected groups. Fairness-aware training may increase, decrease, or redistribute membership vulnerability depending on the algorithm, subgroup, model, and attack [18,21]. Such variation is better interpreted as context dependence than as evidence that disparity is absent. Conversely, non-significance does not establish equal privacy, particularly when groups are small, tail events are rare, or multiplicity correction reduces power.

Many apparent conflicts instead reflect different estimands. Record-level vulnerability is not equivalent to patient-level membership risk; as distinguished in Section 5.4, top-tail composition differs from the probability that a member of a particular group enters that tail; and aggregate ROC-AUC answers a different question from TPR at a prespecified low FPR [2,3,6,7]. Uncalibrated scores may also reflect intrinsic sample difficulty rather than membership-specific behavior [13], while concatenating scores across targets need not control the FPR for each sample [14]. Likewise, within-site auditing distinguishes members among comparable patients, whereas cross-dataset auditing may exploit hospital, device, or preprocessing signals. This distinction affects the subject-level ECG results of Wang et al. [22] and the hospital-source effects examined by Humphries et al. [23]. Bai et al. showed that split artifacts can rival a nominal MIA signal [24], while Shi et al. found that distribution shift can either strengthen or weaken attacks [25]. Findings based on these different contrasts are not necessarily contradictory.

Generalization is therefore limited. Few studies conduct direct patient-level clinical audits, and current evidence spans a restricted set of institutions, tasks, model families, and acquisition pathways. Wang et al. provide useful subject-level ECG evidence, but cross-dataset non-members make participation leakage difficult to separate from dataset-origin recognition [22]. Most studies also focus on membership inference from loss or confidence; whether the same patients remain vulnerable to attribute inference, inversion, or reconstruction is largely unknown. Intersectional validation is sparse, and small cells make both effect estimates and null findings unstable. Jebreel et al. further found that binary vulnerable sets defined at very low FPR may reproduce poorly across runs even when likelihood-ratio rankings are more stable [26]. These limitations require a clear distinction between direct patient-level clinical evidence, clinical record- or subject-level evidence, nonclinical empirical findings, and methodological evidence concerning calibration, bias, or reproducibility.

Because the included studies differed in the protected privacy event, unit of analysis, attacker access, comparator population, operating point, and outcome scale, their estimates were not sufficiently commensurable for quantitative pooling; a structured narrative synthesis was therefore used. Taken together, current evidence supports the existence of heterogeneous and sometimes disparate privacy exposure, but does not establish a universal ranking of vulnerable populations or the stable identification of the same high-risk patients across attacks, models, and clinical settings. Section 6 therefore examines the proposed drivers of these patterns while distinguishing empirical associations from established causal mechanisms.
