# 6. Potential Drivers of Disparate Privacy

The evidence reviewed in the preceding sections shows that privacy exposure can vary substantially across records, patients, and clinical populations. This section examines five candidate explanations for that heterogeneity: selective memorization and generalization behavior; rarity, representation, and distributional differences; model capacity and training procedures; the multiplicity and dependence of patient records; and sample or patient influence. These explanations are related but non-equivalent. The available literature therefore supports treating them as interacting hypotheses whose independent and causal contributions remain to be established.

## 6.1 Memorization and Generalization

Memorization, overfitting, generalization gap, and membership vulnerability are related but non-equivalent. Memorization is example-specific dependence: including a training example changes what the model predicts or assigns high likelihood to beyond what can be inferred from the remaining data. In supervised learning, it can be measured by comparing predictions when an example is included versus excluded; in generative models, it may appear as unusually high exposure of a rare sequence. Overfitting denotes adaptation to training data that impairs performance on unseen data, whereas the generalization gap is an aggregate difference between training and test or population loss. Membership vulnerability is adversarial: it is the ability to distinguish inclusion from exclusion under a specified attack, prior, and access model. A model may therefore memorize selected examples without a large average generalization gap, and memorization does not guarantee successful membership inference.

Yeom et al. provide the clearest formal connection between overfitting and membership inference [10]. For their bounded-loss threshold attack, membership advantage is related to average generalization error; under the conditions of that attack, a positive generalization gap is sufficient to create an exploitable membership signal. Their experiments also associate increasing overfitting with greater attack success. However, this relationship concerns a particular attack and an expectation over training sets and examples, not the vulnerability of every record. Yeom et al. further show that overfitting is not necessary for privacy leakage in general: a learning algorithm can reveal membership while retaining good predictive performance or stability. Consequently, an aggregate association between generalization gap and MIA success neither proves that every vulnerable record was memorized nor guarantees that a small gap eliminates leakage.

Targeted audits reinforce this distinction. Carlini et al. demonstrated effective low-FPR membership inference against a high-accuracy CIFAR-10 model without conspicuous aggregate overfitting, showing that good test performance does not exclude confident inference for a minority of examples [2]. In a different setting, *The Secret Sharer* found that generative sequence models with similar validation performance could differ markedly in their exposure of rare inserted sequences, which could become memorized before conventional overtraining was apparent [29]. Because that study examined synthetic canaries in language models rather than clinical classifiers, its quantitative results should not be transferred to patient records. Its conceptual implication remains relevant: validation performance averages over a distribution and may remain strong while a model assigns exceptional, potentially extractable probability to a small number of unusual training items. Low average generalization error is therefore compatible with an extreme-risk tail.

Sample-level work offers a more precise account of selective dependence. Feldman's long-tail model shows that memorization may be statistically useful, rather than simply erroneous, when data contain many low-frequency subpopulations: fitting rare but informative examples can be necessary for close-to-optimal generalization [27]. Feldman and Zhang operationalize this idea using memorization and influence estimates [28]. Memorization compares the probability of correctly predicting an example when it is included versus excluded, whereas influence measures how that inclusion changes performance on other examples. On non-clinical benchmarks, highly memorized samples included atypical observations, outliers, and mislabeled examples, but removing memorized examples could also reduce test accuracy. Song and Mittal likewise found heterogeneous sample-level privacy-risk scores associated with model sensitivity, class-level generalization, and representation properties [15]. These findings make example-specific dependence a plausible link to membership vulnerability, but they do not make privacy risk, influence, and memorization interchangeable measures.

The clinical results of Knolle et al. are consistent with this account but do not establish it causally [3]. Across seven clinical datasets, some patients exhibited near-perfect membership distinguishability although aggregate attack AUCs remained close to random guessing. These vulnerabilities appeared despite measures intended to limit overfitting and clinically competitive predictive performance; the high-risk tail also expanded for larger model configurations. This pattern is compatible with selective memorization that is invisible to average generalization measures. However, the study estimated membership separability rather than a causal memorization quantity and did not isolate memorization from correlated explanations such as rarity, subgroup representation, record multiplicity, label difficulty, or architecture. High patient-level MIA AUC is therefore evidence of exposure, not proof that the model stored a recoverable copy of the patient's record.

Overall, memorization and generalization are plausible, partially supported explanations for heterogeneous privacy risk, but the evidence remains largely associational at the patient level. Stronger causal inference would require interventions that change example-specific dependence while holding alternative explanations as constant as feasible—for example, leave-one-patient-out or subsampled influence experiments, controlled manipulation of rarity or duplication, training-time tracking of memorization and MIA risk, and matched comparisons of model capacity and regularization. Evidence would be especially persuasive if reducing a patient's measured dependence consistently reduced that patient's membership vulnerability without merely changing attack calibration. Until then, generalization gap should be treated as a system-level warning signal and memorization as a candidate mechanism, not a complete explanation of who becomes high risk. Section 6.2 therefore examines whether rarity, representation, and distributional differences create conditions under which selective memorization and privacy exposure emerge.

## 6.2 Data Rarity, Representation, and Distributional Differences

Rarity arises at several levels that should not be treated as equivalent. Population-group rarity describes a demographic or clinical subgroup contributing relatively few patients or records; diagnosis or class rarity concerns a low-prevalence label; and local record rarity describes an observation with few close analogues in the relevant feature space, even within a large group. Distributional difference is distinct again, arising from institution, device, acquisition protocol, preprocessing, or clinical workflow. Long-tail theory suggests that learning rare subpopulations may require example-specific memorization [27], while influence estimates show that highly memorized examples often include atypical observations [28]. Yet group size is not a proxy for local density: a patient from a small subgroup may have many clinically similar neighbors, whereas an unusual patient may belong to a majority group. These forms of rarity must therefore be measured separately.

The strongest direct clinical evidence remains observational. Knolle et al. found that some underrepresented disease, demographic, insurance, and imaging-protocol categories appeared disproportionately in the highest record-level MIA-risk percentile [3]. This supports an association between representation and extreme exposure but does not isolate low representation as its cause; disease difficulty, label composition, record multiplicity, acquisition context, and model configuration may also shape the tail. Wang et al. similarly reported stronger subject-level participation leakage in small or institution-specific ECG cohorts, whereas larger and more diverse datasets showed lower operational tail risk [22]. However, their cross-dataset non-members originated from different datasets, allowing attacks to exploit institutional, device, resampling, or preprocessing signatures. These findings demonstrate cohort-associated leakage but do not cleanly separate within-institution membership from dataset-origin recognition.

Controlled changes to class representation offer evidence closer to causal intervention, although not at clinical patient level. Truex et al. found greater membership-inference vulnerability for minority classes in skewed datasets, with risk also varying with overfitting [30]. Hu et al. constructed long-tailed CIFAR datasets with controlled imbalance factors and observed increasing low-FPR attack success from head toward tail classes [31]. Tail vulnerability persisted even when long-tailed learning improved tail accuracy and differential privacy reduced overall leakage. These experiments strengthen the claim that changing representation can change class-conditional exposure. Nevertheless, they concern benchmark classes rather than patients, and representation changes also alter optimization, loss, accuracy, and effective overfitting. They do not establish that reducing a clinical subgroup's prevalence will invariably increase every member's risk.

Local rarity shifts attention from group counts to a target's location within the distribution. Long et al. showed that well-generalized models can contain vulnerable records whose inclusion produces distinctive influence not captured by aggregate overfitting; although their experiments included a cancer benchmark, they did not constitute a modern patient-level clinical audit [32]. In a simplified fixed-target game for an empirical-mean mechanism, Azize and Basu characterized optimal leakage as increasing with the target's Mahalanobis distance from the data-generating distribution [33]. DOMIAS instead measures local overfitting in generative models by comparing synthetic-model and reference densities and was particularly effective against uncommon samples [34]. These results support a plausible mechanism: when few comparable observations surround a target, its inclusion may change model behavior more distinctly. However, theoretical distance and generative density ratios are not direct measures of vulnerability in clinical prediction models.

Distributional differences and reference mismatch further complicate interpretation. Humphries et al. showed that dependencies between member and non-member sources, including hospital-specific distributions, can increase measured MIA success relative to an IID evaluation [23]. Shi et al. found no uniform direction: cutout, jitter, Gaussian noise, label shift, and attribute shift could increase or decrease attack performance, sometimes oppositely across attacks [25]. Watson et al. additionally showed that uncalibrated scores can confuse membership signal with intrinsic sample difficulty; low loss may simply indicate that an example is easy even for models trained without it [13]. Audits should therefore use patient-disjoint splits, within-site comparators, site- or protocol-matched reference models, and difficulty calibration. Sensitivity analyses should also condition on diagnosis, acquisition variables, record multiplicity, and the definition of a clinically meaningful neighborhood.

Rarity is therefore better understood as a family of context-dependent hypotheses than as a universal risk factor. Kulynych et al. show that subgroup disparity can be genuine while warning that naive application of membership attacks may overestimate it, particularly for small or poorly calibrated groups [7]. The Privacy Onion Effect, demonstrated on CIFAR benchmarks, further shows that exposure is relative to the remaining training distribution: removing the most vulnerable outliers can expose a new layer of previously safer examples [35]. Consequently, neither group size nor one global outlier score yields a stable ordering of patients across retraining. The available evidence supports rarity, underrepresentation, and distributional distance as plausible amplifiers of privacy risk, but not as universal or independently causal determinants. Direct clinical evidence remains predominantly observational, while controlled manipulations of representation have largely been conducted on non-clinical benchmarks. Section 6.3 therefore examines how model capacity and training procedures may amplify, suppress, or redistribute these risks.

## 6.3 Model Capacity and Training Procedures

Model capacity is multidimensional rather than a simple parameter count. It may reflect model depth and width, architectural family, input resolution, and the use and scale of pretraining. In the principal clinical study, Knolle et al. compared convolutional and transformer-based models of different sizes and found that increasing capacity was associated with a marked expansion of the high-risk tail: larger configurations produced a greater proportion of patients whose membership could be distinguished with very high accuracy, even when aggregate attack performance remained modest [3]. This finding suggests that capacity changes may affect the distribution of privacy exposure rather than merely shift its mean. However, the comparisons did not fully isolate parameter count as the causal factor. Architecture, image resolution, and pretraining also varied across some configurations, including the use of a vision transformer pretrained on a large natural-image dataset. The observed expansion of the extreme-risk tail should therefore be interpreted as an association with the overall model configuration, not as evidence that parameter count alone causes patient-level leakage.

The broader literature likewise provides no basis for assuming a universally monotonic relationship between capacity and privacy. Tan et al. theoretically showed, in an overparameterized linear-regression setting with Gaussian data, that membership-inference vulnerability can increase with the number of model parameters; their experiments in several non-clinical learning settings supported a similar pattern [36]. This provides a plausible explanation for why highly expressive models may encode stronger signals about particular training examples. A complementary analysis by Tan et al. demonstrated that the direction of the relationship can change when regularization is coordinated with capacity [37]. In their theoretical logistic-regression setting, and empirically in neural networks trained with early stopping, larger models could achieve both improved generalization and stronger resistance to membership inference than smaller alternatives. Thus, capacity cannot be evaluated independently of the procedure used to constrain or terminate learning. These results are not direct evidence about clinical patients, but they prevent the clinical association reported by Knolle et al. from being interpreted as a universal scaling law. Capacity is a context-dependent risk modifier rather than an independent or monotonic cause of privacy leakage.

Training duration provides one mechanism through which capacity and regularization may jointly affect exposure. Yeom et al. connected membership-inference success to overfitting and the separation between training and population losses, while also showing that overfitting is not necessary for all forms of membership leakage [10]. Continuing optimization for additional epochs can reduce training losses more rapidly than non-member losses, thereby strengthening the distinction exploited by loss-based attacks. Consistent with this mechanism, Tan et al. observed that membership-inference accuracy increased as training proceeded and member and non-member loss distributions became more separable [37]. Early stopping may limit this separation by selecting an earlier training state, but its effect depends on model size, data distribution, optimization, and the stopping criterion. Similarly, weight decay may restrict effective model complexity, yet its privacy benefit should not be inferred solely from its nominal strength: excessive or poorly matched regularization may reduce utility without protecting the most vulnerable examples. Epoch count, early stopping, and weight decay should therefore be reported jointly and evaluated as components of a training configuration rather than treated as universally protective interventions.

Data augmentation further illustrates why training procedures cannot be assigned a fixed privacy effect. Kaya and Dumitras found that augmentation intensities selected primarily to maximize predictive utility did not substantially mitigate membership inference across their non-clinical image-classification experiments [38]. Stronger augmentation sometimes reduced attack success, but this protection was accompanied by a utility penalty. The authors also found that label smoothing could amplify membership risk, demonstrating that procedures commonly classified as regularizers do not necessarily protect privacy. Moreover, the privacy-utility behavior of high-intensity augmentation resembled that of reducing the number of training epochs: both weakened the membership signal while limiting model fitting. Augmentation may therefore alter privacy through several pathways, including effective training duration, invariance, and example-specific difficulty. Because these results were obtained from general image benchmarks, they motivate clinical sensitivity analyses but do not establish that the same effects will occur for medical images, physiological signals, or longitudinal records.

For patient-level assessment, the central question is not simply whether a larger model has a higher aggregate attack score, but how a training decision changes the full distribution and identity of exposed patients. Capacity could raise vulnerability broadly, expand only the extreme-risk tail, or redistribute risk so that different patients become most exposed. It may also interact with rarity and underrepresentation, producing larger changes for records that have few close neighbors in the training distribution. Knolle et al. provide initial clinical evidence that higher-capacity configurations can expand the high-risk patient tail [3], while the sample-specific analysis of Song and Mittal shows more generally that changes in average privacy risk need not affect all examples equally [15]. Future clinical comparisons should therefore report patient-level risk distributions, tail sizes, rank correlations, and overlap between high-risk sets across matched architectures and training procedures. They should also vary parameter count, resolution, pretraining, epochs, regularization, and augmentation separately wherever feasible. At present, clinical evidence that isolates each of these decisions while repeatedly measuring patient-level vulnerability remains limited. Section 6.4 consequently considers another source of heterogeneous exposure that may interact with model capacity and training: the multiplicity, correlation, and contribution of records belonging to the same patient.

## 6.4 Record Multiplicity, Correlation, and Patient Contribution

Record multiplicity refers to the number of records contributed by patient p, denoted by m_p, whereas contribution weight describes how strongly that patient enters the training objective through record count, sampling frequency, or repeated exposure across epochs. Under record-uniform empirical-risk minimization, the patient's nominal weight is proportional to the fraction of all training records that the patient contributes:

$$
w_p=\frac{m_p}{\sum_q m_q}.
$$

Patient-uniform sampling can remove this mechanical weighting without eliminating the informational effects of repeated records. A third concept, within-patient correlation, captures dependence among images, visits, or physiological signals belonging to the same individual. Patients with equal record counts may therefore contribute very different information: ten nearly identical images do not have the same effective contribution as ten clinically and temporally diverse visits. Multiplicity may affect privacy through three mechanisms. An opportunity effect occurs when patient risk is defined by the maximum record score, because patients with more records have more opportunities to produce an extreme value. A training-influence effect may arise when repeated records give one patient greater weight in the objective or gradient updates. A shared-signature effect may occur when correlated records reinforce stable subject-specific features, including anatomy, chronic disease patterns, or acquisition characteristics. The first mechanism may be partly a measurement artifact, whereas the latter two represent potential changes in what the model learns.

Knolle et al. provide the principal clinical evidence that multiple-record contributions change the appropriate audit [3]. Their target subsets were sampled at patient level, so all records from a patient were included or excluded together; preliminary record-level sampling underestimated attack success when related records remained in training. The study summarized patient vulnerability using the maximum record-level MIA AUC and also showed why record-level differential privacy does not automatically protect a complete patient contribution. However, it did not vary (m_p) independently. A maximum can rise mechanically with record count, while record count may also reflect disease severity, protocol, or follow-up duration. The study therefore establishes the importance of patient-level construction, not a causal dose-response relationship between record count and vulnerability.

Within-patient records also violate the independence assumptions underlying many privacy audits. Repeated observations may share anatomy, biological characteristics, chronic diagnoses, institutional equipment, acquisition protocols, preprocessing pipelines, or temporal context. Humphries et al. showed that statistical dependencies between training examples can substantially increase membership-inference success and that evaluations assuming independent and identically distributed records may underestimate leakage [23]. Their experiments do not directly reproduce longitudinal records belonging to individual clinical patients, so they provide general mechanistic support rather than a clinical estimate of the effect's magnitude. The methodological implications are nonetheless important: training, test, and reference sets should be separated at the patient level; reference-model datasets should preserve realistic dependency structures; and record scores from the same patient should not be treated as independent probabilities. Statistical uncertainty should likewise be estimated through patient-level resampling or cluster bootstrap procedures rather than by independently resampling correlated records.

Subject-level attacks show that exposure can arise from the joint structure of a person's records. Suri et al. infer whether a subject's distribution contributed to federated learning without requiring the exact training records [16]. Li et al. use compactness among a user's embeddings and observed higher recall for users with more training images [17]. Both studies are non-clinical, and the latter concerns person re-identification, so they provide mechanistic support rather than clinical effect estimates. They nevertheless clarify the need to separate records used for training from records available to the attacker:

$$
m_p^{\mathrm{train}}=\text{number of patient records used for training},
$$

$$
q_p^{\mathrm{attack}}=\text{number of patient records available to the attacker}.
$$

Increasing the former may change what the model learns about the patient, whereas increasing the latter may improve only the attacker's statistical power. Their effects should not be conflated.

A monotonic relationship between record count and privacy risk should not be expected. Near-duplicate records may increase a patient's effective training weight, whereas diverse records may improve representation of the patient's underlying distribution and reduce dependence on any single observation. Additional records could consequently reduce record-level identifiability while increasing subject-level distinguishability. Highly correlated records also contribute less independent information than an equal number of heterogeneous records, and a maximum-based patient score can rise with m_p even if the model's treatment of every record remains unchanged. Results from clinical auditing, dependent-data experiments, and subject-level attacks therefore support several competing mechanisms rather than a universal direction of effect [3,16,17,23]. Record multiplicity is a configuration-dependent risk modifier whose effect depends on within-patient correlation, sampling weight, the membership hypothesis, the attacker's auxiliary records, and the patient-level decision rule.

Future studies should vary training record count while separately controlling attacker-held records, within-patient similarity, cohort size, and sampling frequency. They should distinguish complete, partial, and any-contribution hypotheses; calibrate patient statistics for record count and dependence; and compare post hoc aggregation with direct patient-level attacks. Controlled subsampling can test dose response while multivariable analyses address diagnosis, rarity, and institution. Current evidence strongly supports patient-level splitting and dependence-aware auditing, but does not establish whether a larger contribution independently raises or lowers vulnerability. Section 6.5 therefore examines influence as a possible link between patient contribution and model behavior.

## 6.5 Sample Influence and Related Explanatory Measures

Data rarity and record multiplicity describe properties of a patient's contribution, but neither establishes that the contribution materially changed the trained model. A deletion effect addresses this counterfactual more directly. For a record z_i and evaluation quantity T, define

$$
I_i^{\mathrm{del}}(T)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[T(f_{D,\xi})-T(f_{D\setminus\{z_i\},\xi})\right],
$$

where $\xi$ represents training randomness. For patient p, who contributes record set R_p, the corresponding quantity is

$$
I_p^{\mathrm{del}}(T)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[T(f_{D,\xi})-T(f_{D\setminus R_p,\xi})\right].
$$

The sign depends on the direction of T and should be declared; the magnitude alone should be used only when direction is intentionally ignored. Interpretation also depends on T: self-influence concerns change in the patient's own loss or prediction; cross-influence concerns effects on other examples; broader quantities may assess overall or subgroup clinical utility; and attack-oriented influence concerns changes in observable statistics such as confidence or loss. These deletion effects are distinct from influence-function approximations, memorization, membership risk, and intrinsic difficulty. A patient may strongly influence clinical utility without being easily identifiable, or minimally affect aggregate utility while producing a distinctive membership signal.

Influence is nevertheless mechanistically relevant because membership inference compares model behavior under inclusion and exclusion. More precisely, vulnerability depends on the separability of the full in and out distributions of an attacker-observable statistic, not merely on a mean deletion effect. A large effect relative to training variability may improve distinguishability, whereas the same mean shift may provide little evidence when the distributions overlap substantially. Long et al. showed that certain examples can exert distinctive influences that support membership inference even when the model generalizes well, including attacks that query records other than the target [32]. However, their study was principally non-clinical and did not constitute a patient-level privacy audit. Song and Mittal similarly found associations between sample-specific privacy-risk scores, model sensitivity, generalization error, and learned representations [15]. These results were correlational and do not demonstrate that reducing sensitivity necessarily reduces privacy risk. Knolle et al.'s record-specific in and out distributions provide clinical evidence that particular records are distinguishable under a repeated resampling experiment [3]. Because the surrounding training subsets and training randomness also vary, however, this design estimates record-specific membership separability rather than a paired causal deletion effect. Existing evidence therefore makes influence a plausible mechanism of heterogeneous membership vulnerability, but does not establish a universal mapping from influence magnitude to privacy risk.

Explicit leave-one-out retraining provides the most direct operational estimate of a deletion effect: train a model on D, retrain it after removing the target contribution, keep the remaining data and protocol fixed as closely as possible, and compare losses, predictions, attack statistics, and utility across repeated seeds. Feldman and Zhang developed repeated-subsampling estimators of memorization and influence that make related inclusion-exclusion comparisons more scalable [28]. Their results also showed that highly memorized examples can improve predictions for rare test examples, indicating that elevated influence is not necessarily undesirable. In clinical auditing, the complete patient contribution must be removed,

$$
D_{-p}=D\setminus R_p,
$$

rather than deleting one record while retaining correlated observations from the same person. Patient influence also cannot generally be assumed to equal the sum of record influences because correlated records may interact nonlinearly:

$$
I_p^{\mathrm{del}}(T)\neq\sum_{j=1}^{m_p}I_{pj}^{\mathrm{del}}(T).
$$

Direct retraining remains expensive and sensitive to initialization, optimization, augmentation, and changes in the fitted solution. Repeated, preferably paired training designs are consequently required to distinguish systematic patient influence from ordinary training variability.

Influence functions offer a cheaper approximation when exhaustive retraining is infeasible. Koh and Liang estimate how infinitesimally upweighting an example changes parameters and a selected loss or prediction [39]. This traces an output to training data but is not itself a privacy metric, and its classical assumptions do not hold exactly for deep non-convex networks. Patient-level deletion is also a grouped, non-infinitesimal perturbation. Koh et al. found that group-influence estimates could rank removal effects usefully while retaining substantial absolute and relative error [40]. Approximate rankings may therefore screen patients for detailed study, but require validation through actual leave-one-patient-out retraining before they support causal or privacy claims.

Candidate measures include loss, confidence, gradient similarity, sensitivity, memorization, representation distance, local density, leverage, and cross-run disagreement. Each captures only part of the mechanism. Low loss may indicate intrinsic ease rather than leakage [13]; large gradients may reflect label noise; and representation distance may measure rarity without proving an inclusion effect. Influence on utility likewise need not match influence on an attack statistic. Because exposure is relative to the surrounding data—as illustrated by the Privacy Onion Effect [35]—these variables should be analyzed separately and conditionally rather than collapsed into an unvalidated composite score.

Clinical validation should compare patient-level deletion effects with patient-level MIA vulnerability while controlling for rarity, record count, diagnosis, institution, and model capacity. Influence on attack statistics and on clinical utility should be measured separately and repeated across seeds, models, and attacks. Approximate estimators should be benchmarked against leave-one-patient-out retraining in a representative subset. Stronger causal evidence would then test whether downweighting a contribution reduces the same patient's vulnerability without harming other patients or subgroup utility. Influence is thus a plausible bridge between contribution and exposure, but no generic estimator has yet been clinically validated for identifying high-risk patients across training and audit conditions. ## 6.6 Strength of Evidence and Unresolved Causal Questions

The proposed drivers rest on different evidence. Association shows that a characteristic and vulnerability vary together; theory explains how a relationship could arise; controlled benchmarks deliberately manipulate a factor but may not transfer clinically; and causal clinical evidence requires intervention or a credible matched design followed by replication. Knolle et al. provide the strongest direct clinical evidence that exposure is heterogeneous [3], yet comparisons of possible drivers remain largely observational and combine differences in representation, disease, acquisition, model configuration, and contribution size. The literature therefore establishes disparate exposure more convincingly than its causes.

Memorization and generalization have relatively strong theoretical and non-clinical support. Yeom et al. relate specified loss-based attacks to generalization error while showing that overfitting is not necessary for leakage [10]. Long-tail theory and influence experiments explain how unusual examples can be selectively memorized despite good aggregate generalization [27,28]. This is compatible with an extreme-vulnerability tail, but no clinical intervention has shown that reducing one patient's memorization consistently lowers their exposure across attacks without sacrificing useful predictions. Mechanistic support is therefore moderate to strong; clinical causal validation is limited.

Rarity and representation combine clinical association with controlled benchmark evidence. Some underrepresented categories were overrepresented in the clinical extreme tail [3], and class-imbalance experiments show greater tail-class vulnerability in some settings [30,31]. Yet population, diagnosis, and local-record rarity are different and may be confounded by difficulty, label noise, disease severity, institution, protocol, model performance, or record count. Difficulty calibration is therefore essential [13]. The evidence supports rarity as a plausible amplifier, but offers weak clinical evidence of an independent causal effect.

Capacity and training are explicitly context-dependent. Larger clinical configurations were associated with a wider exposed tail, but parameter count, architecture, resolution, and pretraining were not independently isolated [3]. Benchmarks show that overparameterization can increase vulnerability [36], whereas regularization or early stopping can reverse the relationship [37], and augmentation or label smoothing can have mixed effects [38]. Capacity is therefore a conditional modifier rather than an independent, monotonic cause.

Multiplicity, dependency, and influence have a strong conceptual relationship to patient exposure but an unsettled direction. Patient-level sampling is necessary [3], and dependent-data experiments show that ignored correlations can distort MIA estimates [23]; neither result proves that more records necessarily increase vulnerability. Sample-specific exposure is associated with sensitivity or distinctive influence [15,32], but influence approximations can contain substantial grouped-deletion error [39,40]. No clinical influence estimator has yet been validated as a reliable high-risk-patient detector.

Table 6.1 summarizes the resulting evidential position. The categories are descriptive syntheses rather than a formal GRADE-style rating and should be interpreted under the appraisal approach defined in Section 2.4.

| Proposed driver | Direct clinical evidence | Controlled or mechanistic support | Current causal conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Memorization and generalization | Clinical patterns are consistent, but the mechanism was not directly measured | Relatively strong | Plausible mechanism without patient-level causal validation |
| Rarity and representation | Record-level clinical associations | Controlled class-imbalance experiments | Potential amplifier with substantial residual confounding |
| Capacity and training | Association with model configuration | Mixed results across training regimes | Conditional modifier rather than a monotonic cause |
| Multiplicity and correlation | Strong evidence for the correct audit unit | Dependent-data and subject-level evidence | Independent dose-response effect remains unresolved |
| Sample influence | No direct clinical validation | Theoretical, subsampling, and approximation evidence | Candidate mediator not validated for patient-risk identification |

The research agenda should replace broad claims with prespecified causal tests: whether rarity remains predictive after matching on difficulty and record count; whether contribution size matters when correlation and cohort size are fixed; whether parameter count alone expands the tail; and whether reducing patient influence lowers vulnerability across compatible attacks without moving exposure to other patients [35]. Factorial or matched clinical experiments, repeated inclusion-exclusion training, patient-level uncertainty, and external replication are needed. Until then, these drivers should guide hypotheses and stratified audits, not deterministic patient labels. Section 7 turns from explanation to intervention and asks who benefits from protection.
