# 7. Existing Mitigation Strategies: Who Benefits from Protection?

This section evaluates existing privacy mitigations through a patient-level lens. It distinguishes formal guarantees from empirical reductions in attack performance and asks not only whether a method lowers average leakage, but also whether it protects patients who were initially most exposed, reduces subgroup disparities, preserves clinical utility, and avoids transferring risk to others.

**Table 7.1. Comparison of mitigation families considered in Section 7**

| Mitigation family | Mechanism and protection claim | Current patient-level evidence | Principal limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Differential privacy | Contribution clipping, random noise, and accounting; provides a formal `(ε, δ)` guarantee for the declared adjacency relation and protected unit | Direct clinical evidence that stronger record-level DP can shift patient-risk distributions downward [3] | Record-level adjacency does not automatically protect a patient's complete multi-record contribution |
| Regularization and training modifications | Early stopping, weight decay, augmentation, label smoothing, or adversarial regularization; protection is empirical and attack-dependent | Limited direct patient-level evidence; regularly trained clinical models can retain an extreme-risk tail [3] | Effects depend on the attack, data, capacity, training configuration, and utility cost |
| Data and representation interventions | Rebalancing, contribution control, deduplication, synthetic data, or feature modification; usually no formal guarantee | Sparse clinical evidence concerning protection of the same patients before and after intervention | May change representation, redistribute exposure, or reduce rare-group utility without eliminating leakage |
| Distillation and output-access restrictions | Teacher-student transfer, label-only release, rounding, noise, query limits, or monitoring; reduces observable signals or operational attack capacity | Per-example non-clinical evidence shows that leakage can persist through distillation [44] | Students may inherit teacher leakage, and adaptive attacks can exploit restricted interfaces |
| Personalized and risk-adaptive methods | Non-uniform budgets, sampling, clipping, or risk-dependent weighting; formal only for the protected unit and accounting specified | No validated end-to-end clinical patient-level pipeline was identified | Audit error, multi-record adjacency, unequal utility effects, and risk transfer remain unresolved |

## 7.1 Differential Privacy and the Unit of Protection

Differential privacy and empirical attack mitigation answer different questions. Differential privacy constrains how much the distribution of a randomized mechanism's outputs can change when the protected contribution is added to or removed from its input dataset [41]. The guarantee is defined independently of any particular membership-inference attack and therefore applies to adversaries beyond those evaluated experimentally, provided that the implementation and accounting assumptions are valid. By contrast, a reduction in membership-inference attack accuracy, ROC-AUC, or TPR at a specified FPR is an empirical observation conditional on the selected attack, model, reference data, and evaluation protocol. Failed or weakened attacks do not establish differential privacy, while a valid differential-privacy guarantee does not imply that every empirical attack score must equal random guessing. Formal guarantees and empirical audits are therefore complementary rather than interchangeable forms of evidence.

The meaning of the guarantee depends fundamentally on the adjacency relation. Under record-level differential privacy, neighboring datasets differ in one training record. Under patient-level differential privacy, they differ in the complete set Rₚ of records contributed by one patient. These definitions coincide only when every patient contributes exactly one record. Group privacy can extend a record-level guarantee to a patient contributing mₚ records, but with weaker parameters. For pure ε-DP, the resulting bound can deteriorate to approximately mₚε. Under approximate (ε, δ)-DP, both parameters deteriorate according to the applicable group-privacy result, with the accumulated δ also depending on group size and ε [41]. Group privacy is consequently a mathematical fallback, not evidence that a record-level mechanism offers the original privacy budget to the patient's complete contribution.

DP-SGD operationalizes differential privacy during neural-network training [8]. At each iteration, gradients are calculated for sampled training examples and each gradient is clipped to a predetermined L2-norm bound. The clipped gradients are aggregated, and Gaussian noise calibrated to the sensitivity bound is added before the model update. A privacy accountant composes the privacy loss across repeated sampling and optimization steps to obtain final (ε, δ) parameters. Clipping limits how strongly one protected contribution can affect an update, whereas noise makes neighboring datasets harder to distinguish. The final guarantee depends on the sampling procedure, noise multiplier, number of optimization steps, adjacency definition, and correctness of the accountant. Clipping without suitable noise and accounting is not, by itself, a differential-privacy guarantee.

The distinction becomes clinically consequential when one patient contributes multiple correlated records. A record-level private optimizer clips and protects each record separately; it does not necessarily bound the combined contribution of all mₚ records by the same parameters. Knolle et al. found that stronger record-level DP-Adam was associated with lower patient-level membership vulnerability [3]. However, some patients contributed several records, and patient-level scores were derived from the most exposed record. Patient-level attack measurements can therefore exceed a bound interpreted for a one-record change without contradicting the underlying record-level guarantee. The study supports differential privacy as an effective mitigation, but does not demonstrate that every patient received an equivalent patient-level guarantee or that the same parameters applied to the patient's complete contribution. The protected unit must be aligned before formal and empirical results are compared.

Constructing genuine patient-level protection generally requires restructuring training around patients. One principled construction samples patients as units, processes and aggregates each patient's records into a single contribution, clips the norm of that complete contribution, and then adds calibrated noise. A maximum number of records, visits, or local training steps per patient may also be imposed to control contribution size and prevent patients with extensive histories from dominating optimization. Most importantly, the accountant must use patient-sampling probabilities and patient-adjacent datasets. McMahan et al. demonstrated the analogous principle for language modeling by clipping complete user updates and defining adjacency through removal of all examples associated with one user [42]. Their results establish feasibility of user-level protection in a non-clinical setting, but do not determine its utility or fairness consequences for heterogeneous medical populations.

The remaining question is who benefits in practice. Aggregate attack reduction may coexist with residual extreme-risk patients, and a decline in mean patient risk does not demonstrate that the upper tail contracted proportionally. Similarly, a common formal patient-level guarantee constrains worst-case distinguishability under the stated adjacency relation, but does not ensure equal empirical risk, equal absolute risk reduction, or equal clinical accuracy across demographic and clinical groups. Evaluations should therefore report aggregate leakage, the distribution and upper percentiles of patient-level risk, subgroup disparities, and overlap of high-risk patients before and after protection. Utility should be assessed overall and within rare or underrepresented populations, because protection may be accompanied by unequal losses in diagnostic performance. Differential privacy provides a formal guarantee only with respect to the adjacency relation and protected unit used in its definition. Record-level privacy cannot be interpreted automatically as patient-level protection when individuals contribute multiple correlated records. Section 7.2 next examines whether regularization and other training modifications protect the same patients despite lacking an equivalent formal guarantee.

## 7.2 Regularization and Training Modifications

Regularization and training modifications seek to reduce privacy leakage by changing either the learning objective or the trajectory through which a model is optimized. Relevant interventions include early stopping, weight decay, label smoothing, data augmentation, and membership-adversarial regularization. Unlike differential privacy, however, these procedures do not generally impose a formal, attack-independent bound on the effect of an individual's data. A reduction in the success of a particular membership-inference attack therefore constitutes empirical mitigation rather than a general privacy guarantee. Yeom et al. showed that limiting overfitting can reduce the signal available to loss-based attacks because members and non-members become less separable by their losses [10]. Nevertheless, overfitting is not necessary for all forms of membership leakage, and good aggregate generalization cannot establish that every patient is protected.

Early stopping and weight decay are commonly expected to reduce membership leakage by preventing training examples from achieving substantially lower losses than comparable unseen examples. Early stopping limits the number of optimization steps during which member-specific patterns can be fitted, while weight decay constrains effective model complexity. Their effects nevertheless depend on the wider training configuration. Tan et al. demonstrated that increased capacity need not worsen privacy when combined with suitable regularization and early stopping; in some settings, larger models achieved both better generalization and stronger resistance to membership inference [37]. Consequently, neither model size nor a single regularization parameter determines protection independently. Comparisons should also be utility-matched: lowering attack performance by severely degrading diagnostic accuracy does not represent a clinically satisfactory privacy improvement.

The designation regularizer should not itself be interpreted as evidence of privacy protection. Kaya and Dumitras found that label smoothing could increase membership vulnerability despite its conventional role in controlling overconfidence [38]. They also showed that augmentation at intensities normally selected for predictive utility did not consistently mitigate membership inference, whereas sufficiently strong augmentation could reduce attack performance at the cost of task utility. An intervention may compress confidence scores or modify training losses without eliminating other membership signals available to a stronger attacker. Evaluations should therefore retrain and retune the attacker after the intervention is applied rather than testing only an attack designed for the undefended model. Data augmentation is considered more fully in Section 7.3 as a data-based intervention.

Adversarial regularization targets membership leakage more directly. Nasr et al. formulated training as a minimax problem in which a membership adversary attempts to distinguish training members from non-members, while the predictive model is optimized to maintain task performance and reduce the adversary's gain [43]. Their non-clinical benchmark experiments showed that this approach could substantially lower black-box membership-inference success with a limited reduction in predictive accuracy. However, the method does not provide a differential-privacy guarantee: its protection depends on the modelled adversary, optimization procedure, and weight assigned to the privacy-utility objective. A model may learn to resist the adversary used during training while remaining vulnerable to attacks exploiting different signals. Moreover, the available evidence does not establish protection for extreme-risk patients, correlated multi-record contributions, or patient-level membership hypotheses.

The clinically important question is therefore not merely whether regularization lowers average attack accuracy, but which patients benefit. Knolle et al. trained clinical models using measures intended to support generalization, including augmentation, weight decay, learning-rate scheduling, and checkpoint selection, yet still identified a small tail of patients with very high membership vulnerability [3]. This finding demonstrates residual exposure in regularly trained clinical models, but it does not isolate the causal effect of any individual procedure. Similarly, Song and Mittal found heterogeneous changes in sample-specific privacy risk under evaluated defenses: reductions in average risk did not imply uniform improvement, and some samples could retain or acquire relatively high risk [15]. Protection should therefore be assessed through paired patient-level changes rather than inferred solely from a shift in the population mean.

A credible evaluation should combine aggregate ROC-AUC and TPR at low FPR with the median, 95th and 99th percentiles of calibrated patient risk, the size of the extreme-risk tail, and absolute risk reduction for each patient. It should additionally report changes in subgroup disparities, overlap between high-risk sets before and after intervention, and stability across training seeds, attack families, and membership signals. Clinical utility should be measured both overall and within relevant subgroups, using an adaptive attacker retrained against each protected model. These outcomes should not be collapsed into a single average, because an intervention may improve system-level privacy while leaving the most exposed patients unprotected or transferring risk to different patients. Regularization and training modifications can reduce membership signals in some training regimes, but their protection is attack-dependent, non-formal, and potentially heterogeneous across patients. Their effectiveness should be judged by changes in calibrated patient-risk distributions, extreme-risk tails, subgroup disparities, and clinical utility. Section 7.3 accordingly examines whether data- and representation-based interventions provide more targeted protection.

## 7.3 Data- and Representation-Based Interventions

Data- and representation-based interventions seek to change what information is presented to a model or retained in its learned features. Data-based approaches include rebalancing, sampling, augmentation, deduplication, patient-contribution limits, removal of selected records, and replacement or supplementation with synthetic data. Representation-based approaches instead modify learned features so that clinically useful information is preserved while rare, identifying, or patient-specific signals are reduced. Unlike differential privacy, these strategies usually provide no formal bound on how much one patient can affect the trained model. Their value must therefore be assessed empirically, not only through aggregate membership-inference performance but also through changes in the distribution and extreme tail of patient-level risk [3,15].

Rebalancing may use oversampling of rare classes, undersampling of common classes, class-balanced sampling, or loss weighting. Truex et al. and Hu et al. associate class imbalance and long-tailed learning with unequal membership vulnerability, but these findings do not establish oversampling as a privacy remedy [30,31]. A crucial distinction is between the number of distinct patients in a group and the number of training exposures of their records. Repeating records from a small patient set may improve numerical class balance while increasing those patients' contribution weights; recruiting additional independent patients may have a different effect. This remains a causal hypothesis requiring controlled testing. Moreover, subgroup estimates for small populations can be unstable, so apparent improvement should be accompanied by uncertainty intervals and appropriately powered comparisons [7].

Data augmentation expands transformations of existing observations rather than adding independent patients. Kaya and Dumitras found that augmentation intensities chosen primarily for predictive accuracy did not consistently mitigate membership inference, whereas stronger transformations could reduce attack success with a utility cost [38]. Its apparent protection may also change when the attacker is adapted to the augmented training procedure. Clinically, Knolle et al. used augmentation within modern training pipelines yet still observed an extreme-risk tail [3]. This demonstrates residual vulnerability, not the causal failure of augmentation, because its effect was not isolated experimentally. Augmentation should therefore be treated as a configuration-dependent mitigation whose consequences may vary across modalities, transformation types, and correlations among a patient's records.

Contribution control includes removing near duplicates, capping records per patient, patient-balanced sampling, and limiting how frequently one patient appears across epochs. These interventions may reduce genuine training influence, but they may also lower measured risk mechanically by decreasing the number of opportunities for an extreme maximum record score [3]. The two effects are not equivalent. Deleting records labeled high risk is similarly uncertain. The Privacy Onion Effect shows that removing the most exposed examples can reveal a new vulnerable layer among records that previously appeared less exposed [35]. Consequently, removal should be followed by retraining and a complete audit of all remaining patients rather than an evaluation restricted to deleted records or the original high-risk set.

Synthetic data can reduce direct circulation of original clinical records, but it is not automatically anonymous. DOMIAS demonstrates that local overfitting in a generative model can permit inference about membership in its source data, with uncommon samples receiving particular attention [34]. For clinical deployment, auditing should cover three connected objects: the generative model, the released synthetic dataset, and any downstream clinical model trained on it. The central patient-level question is whether synthesis actually reduces leakage for rare patients or reproduces distinctive characteristics that preserve evidence of their contribution. Synthetic data should therefore be treated as a release mechanism requiring renewed privacy assessment, not as proof that privacy has already been achieved.

Representation-based interventions may use representation balancing, feature compression, reduced reliance on patient-specific features, or learning more shared features among clinically similar observations. The heterogeneous sample-specific risks reported by Song and Mittal motivate this direction, while long-tailed learning results indicate that interventions can alter how minority classes are represented [15,31]. Neither body of evidence, however, validates representation modification as patient-level protection. Making an embedding less predictive of a demographic or sensitive attribute does not show that it lacks an individual patient signature, and suppressing subgroup information may also remove clinically useful signals. Direct clinical evidence that such methods reduce calibrated membership risk, especially for the most exposed patients, remains limited [3].

Every intervention should therefore be compared with its baseline under the same membership hypothesis and patient-disjoint construction, using an adaptive attacker retrained after the intervention. Reporting should combine aggregate ROC-AUC and TPR at low FPR with the 95th and 99th percentiles, extreme-tail size, subgroup disparities, and the paired patient-level change from baseline. Studies should report how many patients improve, remain unchanged, or worsen; whether high-risk rankings persist; and whether overall and rare-group clinical utility is preserved. Patient record counts and effective training exposures should also be tracked, while uncertainty in small groups should be quantified [3,7,15]. Data- and representation-based interventions may reduce, redistribute, or obscure privacy exposure rather than eliminate it. Current evidence does not establish that they consistently protect the patients at greatest risk or reduce disparities without creating new high-risk patients. Section 7.4 therefore examines whether distillation and restrictions on model outputs protect the same patients.

## 7.4 Distillation and Output-Access Restrictions

Distillation and output-access restrictions intervene at different points in the release pipeline. Knowledge distillation changes the deployed model by transferring predictive behavior from a teacher to a student, whereas output restrictions change what an external user can observe. Neither approach normally supplies a formal patient-level guarantee comparable to differential privacy. A lower success rate for one membership-inference attack may reflect reduced leakage, loss of a particular attack signal, or increased extraction cost. These outcomes should be distinguished because average attack performance may improve while a small set of patients remains in the extreme-risk tail [3,15].

In conventional distillation, a teacher fT is trained on private data. Transfer inputs are then submitted to the teacher, and its soft labels or logits form a query set from which the student fS is trained. Deploying the student creates three privacy objects: membership in the teacher dataset, membership in any sensitive transfer dataset, and information about teacher examples inherited by the student. These objects require separate hypotheses and audits. The student's lack of direct access to teacher records does not establish their privacy, while use of a sensitive transfer set introduces an additional membership surface.

Jagielski et al. demonstrate that students can retain membership information originating from their teachers [44]. Their LiRA-based attacks evaluated private-teacher, private-student, and self-distillation settings across four non-clinical datasets: CIFAR-10, WikiText-103, Purchase-100, and Texas-100. Although distillation reduced attack performance on average, many teacher examples received little or no per-example privacy benefit, and examples vulnerable in the teacher often remained vulnerable after transfer. Membership could sometimes be inferred indirectly from predictions on student-query examples rather than by querying the teacher on the target record itself. Leakage increased when teacher and student datasets were similar, especially in the presence of near duplicates. The attacks relied on prediction access, but their auxiliary assumptions differed: the end-to-end attack required knowledge of the student training set, whereas alternative attacks used different reference information. Removing direct teacher access therefore does not remove membership evidence. The study does not, however, establish persistence of patient-level risk, multi-record exposure, or the same extreme-risk patients in clinical models.

Output-access restrictions modify the inference interface. Label-only access hides confidence scores; top-k release and rounding reduce their resolution; output perturbation adds noise; query limits cap observations; and monitoring attempts to detect abnormal patterns. Each changes the adversary's information or operational budget, but none necessarily changes what the model learned. Noise or rounding may defeat an attack calibrated to unmodified outputs while remaining vulnerable to an attacker retrained against the protected interface [15]. Query limits and monitoring may raise exploitation cost without lowering latent patient vulnerability, and their effectiveness depends on whether requests can be linked across accounts, devices, or time.

Hiding confidence values is insufficient evidence that membership leakage has disappeared. Choquette-Choo et al. showed that label-only adversaries can exploit predicted-label stability under perturbations or estimate proximity to the decision boundary, using robustness as a membership signal [5]. Confidence masking therefore removes one observable channel but may leave membership-dependent decision behavior intact. Three claims must be reported separately: eliminating leakage from the learned model, concealing the signal used by a previous attack, and increasing the query cost of exploitation. Only the first concerns removal of the underlying membership signal, and even that conclusion requires evaluation against multiple adaptive attacks.

Restrictions can also impose clinically meaningful utility losses. Confidence scores may be needed to assess calibration, communicate uncertainty, set triage thresholds, or rank patients for follow-up. Rounding or perturbation may change sensitivity and specificity at operational thresholds even when discrimination remains similar. These effects should be measured overall and within clinical and demographic subgroups, particularly for rare conditions. Knolle et al. motivate examination of the full distribution of patient exposure [3], but their study should not be cited as direct evidence that distillation or restricted outputs protect clinical models.

Evaluation should compare baseline and protected models under the same membership hypothesis and patient-disjoint construction, with attacks adapted to the new interface. Teacher, student, and any sensitive transfer dataset should be audited separately. Reports should include aggregate ROC-AUC, TPR at low FPR, median and 95th and 99th patient-risk percentiles, extreme-tail size, and paired patient-level change, together with overlap and rank stability among high-risk patients across seeds. Query budgets must be stated, while discrimination, calibration, clinical thresholds, and subgroup utility should be reassessed. Distillation and output-access restrictions can reduce direct information exposure or increase the operational cost of membership inference, but neither establishes that patient-specific membership signals have been eliminated. A student may inherit leakage from its teacher, while label-only attacks may exploit decision behavior after confidence scores are hidden. Section 7.5 therefore examines whether protection can be personalized or adapted according to estimated patient-level vulnerability rather than applied uniformly to all patients.

## 7.5 Existing Personalized and Risk-Adaptive Approaches

Personalized privacy, individual privacy accounting, and risk-adaptive protection describe different operations. Personalized privacy assigns an individual a prespecified budget εₚ, reflecting a stated preference or institutional policy. Individual accounting tracks or upper-bounds the privacy loss accumulated by each contribution, even under a shared mechanism. Risk-adaptive protection instead uses an empirical vulnerability estimate R̂ₚ to select the intervention applied to patient p. Song and Mittal's sample-specific scores illustrate a possible auditing input, whereas differential privacy defines the formal meaning of an assigned budget [15,41]. A patient's privacy preference, formally assigned privacy budget, and empirically estimated membership vulnerability are distinct quantities and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Boenisch et al. provide a formal example of non-uniform assignment through Individualized DP-SGD [45]. IDP-SGD-Sample assigns different Poisson sampling probabilities: examples with larger budgets, and therefore weaker protection requirements, can be sampled more frequently. IDP-SGD-Scale changes per-example clipping bounds so that examples experience different effective signal-to-noise ratios while gradients remain efficiently aggregated. Both mechanisms connect predetermined budgets analytically to DP-SGD parameters and can improve utility relative to assigning every example the most restrictive budget. However, privacy levels are supplied before training rather than selected through membership-inference auditing. The formulation also assumes that each training point is contributed by a different individual. It therefore demonstrates individualized example-level guarantees, not protection for a clinical patient contributing multiple correlated records.

Moving from records to patients requires redefining adjacency and contribution control. Assigning εᵢ to every record i in Rₚ does not automatically give patient p the same guarantee, because removing that patient changes all records in Rₚ. One principled patient-centered construction would sample patients, aggregate their records into one contribution, clip the total contribution, and account for privacy under patient-level adjacency [3,41]. It must also address unequal record counts; otherwise heavily observed patients may contribute more gradient mass or exhaust budgets differently. Individualized DP-SGD could inform such a design, but its example-level analysis cannot simply be relabelled patient-level [45]. The protected unit, contribution bound, and composition rule must be specified before εₚ can be interpreted as a patient guarantee.

A second gap separates patient-level auditing from intervention selection. Song and Mittal estimate heterogeneous sample-specific risk [15], and Knolle et al. identify an extreme tail of clinically exposed patients [3], but neither implements a closed loop in which measured risk determines protection and the resulting model is independently re-audited. A conceptual pipeline is:

$$
\widehat{R}_p \xrightarrow{\;g\;} a_p
\xrightarrow{\;\text{protected training}\;} f^{\mathrm{protected}}
\xrightarrow{\;\text{independent re-audit}\;} \widehat{R}'_p,
\qquad
a_p \in \{\varepsilon_p,q_p,C_p,w_p\}.
$$

Here, aₚ may denote a privacy budget, sampling probability, clipping bound, or contribution weight. The policy g should be prespecified and auditable, and it should incorporate uncertainty and robustness across suitable attacks, seeds, and reference-model constructions rather than acting on one noisy binary label. Data used to select aₚ should be separated from the final evaluation to limit overfitting of the protective policy to the audit itself.

Asiaee et al. provide a closer proof of concept for risk-guided intervention in differentially private synthetic data [46]. REPS spends part of the privacy budget to estimate record outlierness and then reduces the contribution weight of records estimated to be more exposed. Its benefits were clearest in a controlled simulation and smaller or inconsistent across three real tabular datasets, showing dependence on scorer, synthesis, and attack settings. The study is a 2026 preprint, operates at record level, and uses outlierness rather than calibrated patient-level MIA vulnerability; it neither handles multi-record patients nor validates a diagnostic model. Any data-dependent parameter selection must also be included in the privacy accounting or performed safely. REPS is therefore a mechanism-design proof of concept, not evidence that clinical risk-adaptive protection is established.

Adaptation can also create harms. Risk estimates may be unstable across attacks or runs, causing inconsistent treatment of the same patient. Downweighting rare patients may reduce their influence and privacy loss while weakening learning for rare diseases or underrepresented populations. Protecting the current tail may expose a new vulnerable layer after retraining, consistent with the Privacy Onion Effect [35]. Small-group comparisons also carry statistical uncertainty [7]. Moreover, R̂ₚ, εₚ, or the assigned protection category may itself reveal that a patient is unusual or has a sensitive condition. These quantities should be access-controlled and governed explicitly rather than treated as harmless metadata.

Future studies should compare an adaptive policy with a uniform intervention matched for total privacy budget, noise, computational cost, or utility loss. Paired risk changes should be calculated under the same membership hypothesis, attack family, calibration or score scale, and patient construction, with confidence intervals that incorporate training and audit variability. Evaluation should report the 95th and 99th percentiles, extreme-tail size, numbers of patients whose risk decreases or increases, rank correlations, high-risk-set overlap, subgroup disparities, and overall and subgroup utility and calibration. Formal reporting must state the protected unit, individual budgets, contribution bounds, and privacy cost of risk estimation; robustness should span attacks, seeds, model variants, and thresholds. Existing methods show that non-uniform budgets can be implemented and that estimated record characteristics can guide protection. However, preference-based personalization, individualized accounting, and risk-adaptive protection are not equivalent. Among studies identified under this review's eligibility criteria, none validated a complete clinical pipeline that converts calibrated patient-level audits into targeted interventions and independently demonstrates protection of the intended high-risk patients without transferring risk or disproportionately reducing subgroup utility. Section 7.6 therefore examines who benefits from existing mitigations and whether they reduce subgroup disparities.

## 7.6 Effects on High-Risk Patients and Subgroup Disparities

Protection can benefit patients in four non-equivalent senses: lowering mean vulnerability, reducing exposure among those initially most vulnerable, narrowing subgroup disparities, or avoiding increased exposure for anyone else. A lower aggregate attack score establishes none of the latter three outcomes. The baseline high-risk population must be fixed before intervention—preferably in an independent discovery audit—because redefining the highest percentile afterward guarantees a new tail even if absolute exposure falls. Section 8 formalizes individual benefit, residual vulnerability, and disparity reduction; this section synthesizes what existing defenses demonstrate about their beneficiaries.

Knolle et al. provide the strongest direct clinical evidence. Strengthening record-level DP-Adam by lowering ε shifted the distribution of patient-level membership-inference risk downward, showing that differential privacy can reduce empirical patient exposure rather than merely an aggregate attack statistic [3]. Nevertheless, the published analysis did not report paired patient-specific reductions for every privacy level, and extreme-tail subgroup composition was not re-evaluated at each level. At ε = 1, some multi-record patients had patient-level MIA AUC values above the upper bound implied for a one-record change. This does not constitute a violation of the record-level differential-privacy guarantee, because removing a patient changes several records; rather, it demonstrates a mismatch between the protected unit and the patient-level event. Thus, stronger record-level differential privacy reduced the patient-risk distribution, but the study did not establish that initially highest-risk patients or subgroups obtained the largest absolute benefit.

Evidence that differential privacy narrows subgroup disparities is less direct. Kulynych et al. connected differential privacy theoretically to bounds on subgroup vulnerability and disparity; in non-clinical experiments, disparities became statistically undetectable in some settings, but with accuracy losses of approximately 5 to 45 percentage points depending on ε [7]. Truex et al. found greater vulnerability for minority classes in skewed data and showed that the effect of differential privacy depends on the budget, dataset complexity, and model [30]. Hu et al. found that tail-class vulnerability could persist under differential privacy even when long-tailed learning improved rare-class accuracy [31]. A common formal bound can constrain worst-case subgroup vulnerability, but it does not imply equal empirical risk, equal absolute risk reduction, or elimination of the long-tail privacy gap. These findings are mainly non-clinical and class-level rather than patient-level studies of multi-record or intersectional clinical populations.

For empirical defenses, beneficiaries are even less identifiable. Song and Mittal show that apparent protection can be heterogeneous and overstated when attackers are not adapted to the defended model [15]. Jagielski et al. report per-example distillation effects, including examples receiving little or no benefit [44]. Evaluations of regularization, augmentation, and access controls nevertheless emphasize aggregate attack success [38,43] and rarely report paired changes among initially vulnerable samples, clinical tail reduction, or subgroup disparities. They may show that one signal became harder to exploit without establishing who was protected.

Targeted protection does not automatically close this gap. Individualized DP-SGD implements non-uniform budgets, but assigns them from preferences or policy rather than calibrated audit results; it demonstrates personalization, not preferential protection of empirically high-risk patients [45]. REPS uses estimated outlierness to guide differentially private synthetic-data generation, yet its results across Breast Cancer, Adult, and German Credit were dataset-dependent, and it remains record-level rather than clinical patient-level evidence [46]. Downweighting an unusual record may reduce its influence while weakening representation of a rare condition. The Privacy Onion Effect also shows that removing currently vulnerable samples can expose a previously obscured vulnerable layer [35]. This demonstrates possible risk transfer, not that every targeted intervention causes it. Targeted protection should therefore be evaluated as redistribution across patients, not only reduction among selected records.

Establishing who benefited requires paired audits of the same patients before and after mitigation, with training subsets and seeds matched where feasible. The membership hypothesis, protected unit, vulnerability scale, and reference construction must remain comparable, while the attacker is adapted to the intervention. Reports should fix the baseline high-risk set independently, quantify uncertainty, use patient-level resampling for multi-record data, and distinguish meaningful worsening from noisy point-estimate changes. Required outcomes include benefit and residual vulnerability among initially exposed patients, the fraction that worsened, subgroup-specific protection, tail overlap, and robustness across attacks and seeds. Section 8 defines these quantities and incorporates clinical utility.

Overall, differential privacy and several empirical mitigations can reduce membership leakage, and clinical evidence indicates that stronger differential privacy can shift patient-level vulnerability downward. Evidence remains limited that existing protections preferentially benefit initially highest-risk patients, consistently shrink clinical subgroup disparities, and avoid transferring exposure. Most subgroup evidence is non-clinical, many defense evaluations remain aggregate, and emerging adaptive methods have not been validated in multi-record clinical populations. Section 8 therefore examines whether privacy gains justify their utility and fairness costs.
